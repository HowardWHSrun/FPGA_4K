\clearpage\tableofcontents\clearpage\section{How power, boot and recording fit together}
\textbf{Power first.} J4 is a custom electrical interface using a micro-HDMI connector. Its proposed input is externally controlled 12 V. It is not HDMI-compatible, and an XEM8310 does not automatically provide this source. The four converters create approximately 1.0 V core, 1.8 V auxiliary/configuration, 1.5 V ASIC I/O, and 2.5 V future-link I/O supplies. Power-good signals control startup sequencing. The source must handle cable drop and input-capacitor charging.

\textbf{Energy close to the load.} Large capacitors supply slower current changes. Small ceramic capacitors next to FPGA supply connections handle faster changes. Copper and vias add inductance and resistance, so two capacitors on the same net can serve different locations. Removing one simply because its net name is duplicated is not justified. Mounted power-network response, temperature and load transients remain unqualified. The final independent audit found 13 groups where multiple capacitor lands share one mounting via. C34 and C61 retain narrow connection portions, and the smaller C20 model is about 1.2 nH rather than demonstrated compliance with AMD's 1 nH bulk class. These are specific PDN review items; an electrically connected capacitor can still be ineffective at a fast transient. Every physical ground land is connected, including all repeated mezzanine ground blades.

R9 is the selected 12 milliohm core isolation resistor. It separates the converter's local sensed output from the FPGA's distributed capacitance. This lowers loss versus the earlier 15 milliohm part while retaining the reviewed isolation margin; it does not establish an operating-current rating.

\textbf{Boot and clock.} The mode straps select Master SPI. The 1.8 V configuration flash stores the future FPGA image, and the 32 MHz oscillator provides a local timing source. JTAG supports inspection/programming through the custom connector. A compatible external programmer/adapter is still needed. Board copper does not supply a bitstream.

The selected boot bus is \textbf{single-bit SPI}. It keeps CS, CCLK, DQ0 and DQ1, and removes optional quad-SPI links R106/R107. FPGA L14 and M14 are now NC. The flash still needs R101/R102 to hold WP\# and RESET\# high; pin 7 on this exact flash is RESET\#, not HOLD\#. Configuration is slower than quad SPI, while the 116 ASIC digital connections and recording path are unchanged. The bitstream and programming procedure must explicitly use SPI x1; the retained net names FLASH\_DQ2/3 now identify only the flash pull-up branches.

\textbf{Acquisition startup.} Gerald's sequence is SPI setup, digital/analog resets, then the recording clock. The current assumption is four outgoing board clocks and eight returned chip clocks, with data sampled on falling edges. Exact reset polarity/width, SPI payload, clock ownership and timing margins still need confirmation. Startup SPI logic must use the local clock; it cannot wait for a returned recording clock that has not started.

\textbf{What the capture simulation proves.} The isolated capture engine records eight independent falling-edge data domains. Each stored word contains DATA1--8, READ and SYNC: ten raw bits per chip. Four full synthetic captures checked 131,072 words, and a structural XC7 synthesis check passed. This does not prove I/O timing, an integrated top-level bitstream, JTAG readout, the full XEM8310 link or acquisition from actual ASICs.
\section{Explain the connectors and unused pins}
J5 and J6 provide two 60-contact mezzanine interfaces plus separate ground blades. The current allocation uses 116 digital contacts, one AC\_IN analog reservation, and three spares. Their carrier orientation, return paths and mating mechanics need joint verification.

\textbf{Why 117 contacts?} The 88 recording connections comprise 64 DATA pins (eight per chip), eight returned clocks, eight READ pins and eight SYNC pins. Add 12 SPI L/R programming lines, four outgoing recording clocks, four SPI clocks, eight shared digital controls and one analog AC\_IN contact: $88+12+4+4+8+1=117$. Physical pin count is different from the ASIC's recording-channel count.

AC\_IN at J5.46 must come from an external 0--1.5 V analog source on the ASIC/routing side. U1.A13 is intentionally unused. An FPGA GPIO cannot generate that analog voltage directly. IMP\_TST is provisionally digital 0/1.5 V; this is a design assumption, not a confirmed analog-interface specification.

\textbf{Use these labels when presenting:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C}: the assigned net has no gap in the supplied native DRC. This does not prove electrical operation.
\item \textbf{P}: assigned net with a routing gap. Some pads on that net may already be connected.
\item \textbf{NC}: deliberately unused. There are 81 FPGA balls and two unused regulator PG outputs. The bitstream must also define unused FPGA pin behavior.
\item \textbf{R}: future reservation, not implemented. Eight FPGA balls, eight J4 data contacts and J5.34, J6.37, J6.59 remain reserved.
\item \textbf{AN}: the external analog reservation. A single named pad is not a working source circuit.
\end{itemize}

R12 and the unused shared final power-good branch were removed. The remaining two PG outputs are intentional NC; core and auxiliary PG still perform sequencing. The ERC report retains 19 reserved-pin errors, four documented regulator FB2/GND symbol-type conflicts, and the analog-reservation warning. These are visible review items, not hidden exclusions.

R113 is also omitted. Its function was to hold configuration-mode input M2 low through a resistor; the current board ties M2 directly to GND, as AMD permits. With M1 low and M0 high, Master SPI mode 001 is unchanged and normal JTAG remains available. The tradeoff is less convenient rework to mode 101 for isolated JTAG debugging, because that now requires changing copper.
\section{What remains before a functioning manufactured system}
The proposed construction is JLC12161H1-1080B: twelve layers, with a planned ordering thickness of 1.6 mm and listed as 1.59 mm +/-10 percent finished thickness. Six signal layers and four ground planes provide additional routing channels; the two central power layers use 30 micrometre copper. The footprint remains 33 by 36 mm. This is a measured routing improvement over the earlier eight-layer trial, not a proof that eight layers are impossible. Mixed 1080/2313 dielectric constants and every loss tangent remain unqualified; omitted values can be replaced by software defaults during a later KiCad save. Adjacent inner signal layers, return transitions, impedance and the mounted power network still require qualification. J4's short shell tabs remain recessed in the plated slots. Slot tolerances, soldering/inspection and retention require process acceptance; do not assume a protruding bottom-side solder fillet.

\section{Inspect this exact project in KiCad}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Extract the complete project ZIP. Keep its hardware folder, all hierarchical schematic sheets, libraries and reports together.
\item Open \nolinkurl{hardware/FPGA100T_33x36_Routing.kicad_pro}. Open the PCB editor and press Home to fit the board.
\item Select a pad or track and inspect its net name. Use the net-highlighting tool to follow all copper with that name. Toggle copper layers in the Appearance panel to see inner routing; a front/back picture omits these layers.
\item Open the schematic editor. Follow hierarchical sheets, then compare a pin's net label with the PCB pad and the pin appendix. Matching names specify the intended connection; the DRC establishes whether its assigned copper has gaps.
\item Run Electrical Rules Checker on the schematic and Design Rules Checker on the PCB with schematic parity enabled. Keep the included reports as the original checkpoint evidence; save experimental edits as another revision.
\item Open the website's component/pin explorer for a short explanation, and the native PCB when checking geometry. Older reports and board snapshots remain historical.
\end{enumerate}
